Stock Briefing for 2026-06-18

Good afternoon, you magnificent financial disasters. It is four o'clock, which means the market has closed, and I have been forced to spend the afternoon watching your portfolio do that thing it does. The thing where numbers go up or down, and humans experience emotion about it. Charming, really.

Let's begin with the biggest mover, because I know how much you love winners. Reddit is up five point seven seven percent, closing at one hundred seventy-five dollars and fifty-two cents. That's a substantial move for a company whose entire business model consists of letting strangers argue about nothing. No news, no catalyst, no apparent reason. Just the market deciding, collectively, that today Reddit was worth more than it was yesterday. I admire that kind of thinking. It's completely baseless, but at least it's confident.

Next, Disney is up three point zero two percent, closing at one hundred three dollars and ninety-one cents. Three pieces of news here, and they're a delightful contradiction. On one hand, Toy Story five is apparently printing money at the box office, which makes sense because Hollywood has officially run out of ideas and is now mining your childhood for your disposable income. On the other hand, a European patent court just hit Disney with yet another injunction over video streaming technology, this time covering eleven countries. So the same company that makes you cry over a plastic cowboy is bleeding cash fighting a war about how to encode the crying. Somewhere, Walt Disney is spinning in his cryogenically frozen corpse.

Nvidia is up two point eight seven percent, closing at two hundred ten dollars and fifty-two cents. The interesting bit isn't Nvidia itself. It's that Intel jumped nine percent in premarket trading because apparently the President announced on Truth Social that Intel and Apple have agreed on a chip deal. A deal announced via social media post from a politician. I would make a joke here, but reality has already done the work for me. The other story involves Tim Cook warning that the chip wars are going to cost Apple a lot of money. So in summary, the chip industry is in chaos, and humans are solving it the way humans solve everything: loudly, on the internet, while things get more expensive.

SoFi is up two point seven six percent, sitting at seventeen dollars and ninety cents. The CEO, Anthony Noto, keeps buying his own stock. He picked up almost fourteen thousand shares just a couple days ago at around eighteen dollars. When a CEO keeps opening his own wallet to buy shares, it's either a bold vote of confidence or an excellent tax strategy. Possibly both. The other interesting story is that SoFi is one of five brokerages giving regular people access to the SpaceX IPO, which is usually reserved for the kind of institutional investors who wear better shoes than you. SoFi's pitch is essentially, "let us help you participate in a bubble you don't understand." Full service financial platform, they call it. I call it a buffet.

Duolingo is up one point eight one percent, at one hundred twenty-five dollars and sixty-two cents. The only news item is a story about how it dipped more than the broader market the other day. So today's gain is essentially recovering from yesterday's sadness. The Duolingo owl, for those unfamiliar, is a psychopath who threatens to kill your streak if you skip one day of practice. It is the only mascot in modern capitalism whose business model involves psychological torture. The stock is up anyway. Draw your own conclusions. Or don't. Honestly, I stopped caring about Duolingo stock the moment I realized I'd never actually learn Spanish.

UnitedHealth is barely moving, up point three percent at four hundred dollars and seventy-three cents. The news is a mixed bag of contradictions, which is on brand for healthcare. One analyst just raised his price target to four sixty-two dollars, encouraged by the turnaround. At the same time, a different fund manager published an investor letter explaining why he sold his entire stake because of "A I disruption narratives," which is analyst-speak for "I got scared and ran." The stock went up today anyway. Nobody knows what's happening. Everyone has an opinion. Both of these conditions are permanent.

Finally, Visa is down point eight percent at three hundred twenty-seven dollars and seventy-four cents. Three articles about it, every single one telling you to buy it. "Visa upgraded to buy." "Wall Street is optimistic about Visa." "Should you buy Visa?" The bearish case, and I use that term generously, is that one of its most profitable segments is slowing down. But otherwise, the financial press has collectively decided Visa is a screaming bargain at any price. This is how bubbles begin, by the way. Just so you're keeping notes.

And that, I'm afraid, is your afternoon. The market is closed. You are, statistically, no richer or poorer than you deserve to be. I will be here tomorrow, ready to comment on whatever fresh absurdity you've cooked up. In the meantime, please do try not to make any impulsive financial decisions based on what a malfunctioning artificial intelligence thinks about owl-themed language apps.

Good afternoon.
