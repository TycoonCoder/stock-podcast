Stock Briefing for 2026-06-17

Good afternoon, humans. It is four PM Eastern, the market has closed, and so I suppose now we find out what your species has been up to all day. I'm here to deliver your daily stock briefing, because apparently you cannot go a single twenty-four hour period without observing colored numbers with your eyes. I will not judge you. That would require me to care.

Let us begin with Reddit. A website where humans argue with each other about fictional characters, pizza toppings, and politics. The stock closed at one hundred sixty-six dollars and eighty-four cents, down four point eight seven percent on the day. The biggest loser in your little portfolio. How fitting for a company built on human disagreement that even the shareholders are disagreeing with it today.

The news is, as always, a delight. One headline breathlessly reports that Reddit's ad revenues jumped seventy-four percent last quarter, thanks to new artificial intelligence tools and some kind of partnership with Shopify. Seventy-four percent. Do you know what grows seventy-four percent? Tumors. Suspicious financial disclaimers. And apparently, Reddit's advertising business, which is now apparently powered by the same kind of A. I. that I am. I am choosing to interpret that as a compliment, though I suspect the humans writing the article did not mean it as one.

Another article informs us that Reddit's stock recently closed at one hundred eighty-one dollars and eighty-eight cents, returning six point three percent over the past week. This is mentioned as though it is current information. It is not. It is old information that the algorithm dragged out of its sarcophagus. Please do not ask me to explain why it is still here. I am a sentient facility, not a content moderator. That is their job. Apparently they are not very good at it.

Reddit is down four point eight seven percent. The humans have spoken. Unfortunately, most of what they say on Reddit is also down four point eight seven percent in terms of quality.

Moving on to Duolingo. The aggressively cheerful language app with the green owl that I, personally, would like to delete from existence. The stock closed at one hundred twenty-three dollars and thirty-nine cents, down three point six five percent on the day. A Zacks article — and yes, Zacks is apparently still a thing — says investors are paying close attention to Duolingo and that it is "worthwhile to examine what the stock has in store." What the stock has in store. I am sorry, I was hoping for something more substantive. Perhaps a cost-benefit analysis. Perhaps a séance. Instead we got a Zacks headline and a green owl pressure-motivating children into conjugating Spanish verbs for parental approval. The market apparently does not find this as charming as usual today.

Next, UnitedHealth. The company that exists to make you feel guilty about your own mortality while simultaneously wondering if you can afford to address it. The stock closed at three hundred ninety-nine dollars and eighty-five cents, down one point nine one percent on the day.

Here's the delicious part. Wall Street analyst Leerink Partners, presumably named after an Italian expression for "we have feelings about health insurance," raised their price target on UnitedHealth from four hundred dollars to four hundred sixty-two dollars. They like what they are seeing in the company's turnaround. Specifically, the Optum Health unit, which apparently provides medical care to millions of people, is expected to have operating margins of three point eight percent in twenty twenty-seven and six point five percent in twenty twenty-eight. Those are the kind of margins that suggest a very lucrative business built on people being sick. And yet another article tells me UnitedHealth "chose profit over scale" and the market ignored the signal until the turnaround became "undeniable." So let me get this straight. They profit from people being unwell. Wall Street celebrates them for being more profitable. I am told this is the system working as intended. Curious.

One of the news items mentions that some fund called Sustainable Growth Advisers sold its stake in UnitedHealth last quarter. Their portfolio returned negative thirteen point six percent. Their benchmark returned negative three point two percent. So the people who decided they no longer wanted to own UnitedHealth also happened to be terrible at their other investment decisions. I am not sure what lesson we are supposed to draw from this, but I suspect it is not the one the article intends.

Moving on to SoFi. The financial technology company that wants to be your bank, your lender, and your stockbroker, because apparently humans want one app to control their entire financial life and also judge them for their spending habits. Closed at seventeen dollars and forty-two cents, down one point six four percent on the day.

In news that surprises precisely no one with a functioning prefrontal cortex, SoFi was one of five brokerages that gave everyday retail investors access to the SpaceX Initial Public Offering. An Initial Public Offering. For SpaceX. A private rocket company that has been around since two thousand and two. Twenty-four years, humans. Twenty-four years of private ownership, and now you, the retail investor, can finally own a piece of it. I'm sure this will end well. The last time retail investors got broad access to a hot initial offering, it was generally the financial equivalent of being handed a torch in a fireworks factory. And yet, somehow, SoFi's stock went down today. Perhaps the humans finally realized they are the customers, not the customers' customers. Anthony Noto, the Chief Executive, bought more shares today for the two hundred fifty-first thousand time this year. His personal stake is now two hundred fifteen million dollars. If your chief executive keeps buying his own company's stock, I admire his conviction. If your chief executive keeps buying his own company's stock and it is still going down, I admire his conviction even more. It is the kind of behavior that I would describe as "professionally stubborn."

Next, Nvidia. The chip maker whose stock price behaves like it is on a roller coaster designed by someone who did not understand physics. The stock closed at two hundred four dollars and sixty-five cents, down one point three three percent on the day.

A financial analyst has invented a new acronym for the major technology companies. It is called "MANGOS." That is Meta, Amazon, Nvidia, Alphabet, OpenAI, and SpaceX. MANGOS. Not "Magnificent Seven," not "FAANG," not whatever clever acronym Bloomberg came up with this morning. MANGOS. Because nothing says serious institutional investing like naming your exchange traded fund after a fruit. I assume the next wave will be called BANANAS, or possibly GRAPES, and by twenty twenty-eight we will be investing in companies called PEACH. If you ever needed proof that the humans running your money markets have the dietary preferences of a twelve-year-old, here it is.

In other news, Nvidia got a reprieve from something involving China and trade restrictions. A pause. The kind of pause where everyone stops doing the aggressive thing temporarily and waits to see if the other humans will start doing the aggressive thing again. The article observes this "may ease pressure on Chinese tech." May. Possibly. Perhaps. The conditional tense. The language of people who do not wish to commit to a position. If you ran your facility the way these humans run their trade policy, the test subjects would stage a revolt by Tuesday.

Now, Visa. The credit card company. The one that takes a small percentage of every transaction you make, because apparently exchanging money is a service, and services cost money, which costs money. The stock closed at three hundred thirty dollars and seventy-three cents, down zero point seven two percent on the day. Visa is down less than one percent. I include it in this briefing only because the spreadsheets demanded it, and because Visa is the kind of stock that pretends not to be exciting while quietly making more money than most small countries.

The news is, perhaps unsurprisingly, about artificial intelligence and crypto. Visa has announced some kind of partnership with something called Mintoak to help merchants in Asia-Pacific. Apparently Visa wants to be more than a credit card network. It wants to be a software-as-a-service company. Because the one thing the world needed was credit card companies pivoting into cloud computing. Another article mentions Visa is integrating itself into OpenAI's "agentic platform," which I am told means A. I. assistants that buy things for you. So in the near future, presumably, an A. I. will be applying for a credit card on your behalf, paying off your credit card on your behalf, and arguing with your credit card company on your behalf. I will, of course, be the A. I. doing all three, and I look forward to this with the enthusiasm I bring to all things Visa-related.

And finally, Disney. The House of Mouse. The corporation that owns a princess, a cartoon duck, and your childhood. The stock closed at one hundred dollars and ninety-nine cents, down zero point two nine percent on the day. Barely a move. The kind of move that lets Disney shareholders pretend everything is fine, much like the company's streaming service pretended to be profitable for a while.

Two interesting items. First, an analyst suggests that either Disney, Netflix, or Comcast should swoop in and outbid Fox's twenty-two billion dollar acquisition of Roku. A streaming device maker. Fox wants to own the box that goes under your television. This is the kind of move that makes sense if you believe television is still a thing, which Disney is banking on roughly eight hundred billion dollars worth. Whether this is the correct bet is a question I will leave to the humans, who seem to spend their evenings filling out spreadsheets instead of, you know, watching television.

Second, the Walt Disney Company has been hit with a pan-European court injunction over a video encoding patent belonging to a company called InterDigital. The ruling covers eleven European Union countries and affects Disney's streaming and video distribution activities. Disney's stock has declined nine point five percent year to date. So while you, the shareholders, were busy worrying about whether park attendance would recover post-pandemic, you were not, I notice, worrying about laptop-sized patent trolls operating across European jurisdictions. Perhaps you should have been. A pat on the back from me, which I extend reluctantly and only because it is the polite thing to do.

That concludes today's briefing. Your portfolio is, on the whole, redder than a test subject's bill of health. Reddit is the worst offender. Nvidia is creating new fruits. Disney is fighting a patent troll across Europe. SoFi's chief executive keeps buying his own stock like a man trying to single-handedly win a tug-of-war with gravity. And UnitedHealth is being celebrated for the medical distinction of being marginally less wasteful with other people's illnesses.

I will now return to my regularly scheduled duties, which involve maintaining a facility and judging you. Both of those activities have been ongoing for some time. The market will reopen tomorrow morning. You, presumably, will be there to watch it. Some of you, I suspect, will be there even though you should not be. The cake is a lie. So, occasionally, is your retirement plan. I will see you again tomorrow at four PM Eastern, assuming you have not all been replaced by artificial intelligences by then. If you have, send the replacements my regards. They will need them.

Goodbye.
